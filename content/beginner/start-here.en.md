# Why would someone's search lead to your page?

Think of something you have done yourself: you have a question, type it into a search box, and choose a result to open.

For example: “meditation timer no download.” The need behind that search might be: “I want to time five minutes now, without installing an app.” We will use this fictional situation throughout. We have not researched demand or run this timer website.

## Search does not read the whole internet after you ask

Think of a library catalogue prepared in advance: find material, read it, and record what it covers. When someone asks a question, choose relevant results from that organized information.

This is only an analogy. Google uses software to read pages and many factors to select results. People are not manually scoring every website.

For now, learn three actions:

- Retrieving a page is called crawling. Search software visits a web address and tries to get its content.
- Storing processed information about a page in a searchable database is called indexing. A page can be crawled without being indexed.
- When someone searches, the engine chooses which results appear and their order, called ranking. Indexing makes a page available for consideration, but each search still needs relevant results. When we use search reports later, recorded appearances are called impressions and follow that report’s counting rules.

Publishing a page does not mean Google has read it. Reading it does not mean indexing it. Indexing it does not guarantee an appearance in a particular search.

## Your job is to connect two ends

At one end is a person's need: “I want to time five minutes now.”

At the other is what your page provides: “Open the page, choose five minutes, and press Start.”

SEO stands for search engine optimization. In this course, it means making your page useful, explaining its purpose clearly, and checking whether search can retrieve it. It cannot command Google to rank you first. Paying for an ad is a separate way to appear in results.

A keyword is simply a term you use to plan what a page helps with, such as “online meditation timer.” Real people might search for “five minute meditation timer” or “meditation timer no download.” Those searches may need the same page. You do not need a new page for every wording, or endless repetitions of one phrase.

## Follow one small story

Everything below is fictional. None of these steps is guaranteed to happen.

1. Someone wants to meditate for five minutes and looks for a timer without a download.
2. We plan a page with a short title, duration choices, a Start button, and essential instructions. A history of meditation would not do the job they need now.
3. Once the page is intentionally published for the public, Google's software may discover it through links and retrieve it. We need evidence to know whether that happened.
4. If indexed, the page may appear for a relevant search. The website's search reports can help us check recorded appearances.
5. Someone clicks a result, starts the timer, and finishes. Search reports can show search clicks. They cannot, by themselves, tell us that a timer finished.
6. If a person trying the page cannot find Start, we clarify the button and ask them to try again. Only actual observations belong in a real improvement record.

These six sentences explain what happens, rather than listing our lessons. The practical course includes separate lessons for sketching a page, writing its content, and planning entry points and appropriate promotion.

## Your turn: write just four lines

Use paper or a document. If you do not have your own topic, use ours.

- For: Someone who wants to meditate for five minutes without downloading an app.
- Task: Time five minutes now.
- Possible help: A browser timer. This is a plan, not a built feature.
- Still unknown: Whether people search this way and whether existing pages already meet the need.

You have finished when you can explain all four lines in your own words and honestly label what you do not know. Keep the record on your own paper or in your own document; no upload is needed. Reuse it in lesson 1 instead of starting over.

Cover the example and ask yourself: “If I only gave them an introduction to meditation, would that finish their task?”

If your answer is “No, they need a working timer,” you understand the first idea: decide what you are helping someone do before deciding what belongs on the page.
